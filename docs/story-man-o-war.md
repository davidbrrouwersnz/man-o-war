## The text

### `look` — Look closely

The whole thing is glass. It stands about 28 centimetres tall. Two long tentacles hang from it,
and seven shorter ones. You can count them. It rests on a green wire rod.

This is a Portuguese man o' war. In New Zealand waters it is better known as the bluebottle. Its
other name comes from its resemblance to an 18th-century Portuguese sailing warship.

### `not-one-animal` — Not one animal

A man o' war is related to true jellyfish. But it is not one. And it is not a single creature
either.

It is a colonial organism, made up of many individual organisms called zooids. Zooids stay
connected as they grow. Each one has its own specialised job. None of them can survive alone.

### `float-and-sail` — A float, and a sail

Look at the top section. In a living man o' war that part does two jobs at once. It is a float,
and it is a sail. They travel on the currents and the wind.

Every man o' war is either left-handed or right-handed, depending on which way that sail curves.

The tentacles below can reach ten metres. They catch small crustaceans and fish.

## Pronunciation lexicon

Fixed here, never by changing the words (§5, §13).

| Token | Respelling |
|---|---|
| zooids | ZOH-oyds — engines flatten it to "zoids" |
| man o' war | man oh war — the apostrophe causes a stumble; occurs four times |
| 28 centimetres | twenty-eight centimetres |
| 18th-century | eighteenth-century |

Set the voice to a New Zealand or British English variant. A US voice stresses *centimetres*
differently.

## What the merge did

**Taken from the audio, not the story:**

- *"You can count them."* / *"Look at the top section."* These are the look cues. They were
  audio-only in v1, which meant the majority of visitors — who never press play — got **no
  instruction to look closely at all**. Printing them fixes a real gap rather than costing
  anything. Both survive the presence test below: you can count tentacles and find the top section
  in a photograph just as well as in a case.

**Cut, because it assumed the object was in front of you:**

- *"Take a moment to look at it. Everything you can see is glass."* Only one object in the
  collection is on display, that display sits on a lease ending mid-2028, and the other 127 are in
  storage **[V]**. A line that presumes physical presence is wrong for almost every object almost
  all of the time. *"The whole thing is glass"* is a fact about the object and holds whether the
  reader is standing at the case, sitting at home, or reading in 2031.
- The audio's shorter sentences throughout. *"But it is not one. And it is not a single creature
  either."* beats the story's comma-spliced version on the ear and loses nothing on the page.
- *"In a living man o' war that part does two jobs at once."* The story said "the top section works
  as two things at once", which is ambiguous about whether it means the glass or the animal. The
  audio's phrasing resolves it, and this object needs that distinction constantly.

**Cut, because it named its own medium:**

- *"Look at the tentacles again as you hear that"* — the last four words fail rule 1. The line
  belongs in layer 3, beside the eggshell-thin glass it was pointing at, without them.

**Kept as numerals**, against v1's audio which spelled everything out: *28*, *18th-century*.
Print style wants numerals; the lexicon handles the voice. *Ten metres* and *seven shorter ones*
stay as words because that is also correct print style — the rule is never to change a word **for**
the audio, not to prefer digits.

**Changed contractions to full forms** — *it is* rather than *it's*. Reads as deliberate on the
page and is marginally clearer aloud. Reversible; a preference, not a rule.

## What to check before this becomes the template

1. **Read it aloud, then read it silently.** If either feels wrong, rewrite the sentence — do not
   split the text (§6).
2. **Does the direct address survive on the page?** *"You can count them"* is the test case. If it
   reads oddly with no one speaking, that is the whole standard failing and we need to know now,
   at one object, rather than at 128.
3. **231 words against v1's 499.** The object story is shorter than expected because five of the
   eight sections moved to shared layers. Check that the object page still feels substantial with
   this much text and a large image — the depth-cliff test in §20 assumes it does.
4. **The zooid claim.** v1's source notes record that *"each has its own specialised job"* and
   *"none can survive alone"* were taken from the Hula Skirt Siphonophore entry on the same Museum
   page, not the man o' war entry — *"a nearby definition applied across entries."* It is textbook
   and almost certainly fine, but v1 thought it worth writing down and it should survive into
   layer 5 rather than evaporate.

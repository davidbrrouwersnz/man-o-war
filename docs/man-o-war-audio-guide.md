```text
Take a moment to look at it. Everything you can see is glass. It stands about twenty-eight centimetres tall. Two long tentacles hang from it, and seven shorter ones. You can count them. The whole model rests on a green wire rod.

This is a Portuguese man o' war. In New Zealand waters, it's better known as the bluebottle. Its other name comes from its resemblance to a Portuguese sailing warship of the eighteenth century.

A man o' war is related to true jellyfish. But it isn't one. And it isn't a single creature either.

It's a colonial organism, made up of many individual organisms called zooids. Zooids stay connected as they grow. Each one has its own specialised job. And none of them can survive alone.

Look at the top section. In a living man o' war, that part does two jobs at once. It's a float, and it's a sail. They travel on the currents and the wind.

And they're either left-handed or right-handed, depending on which way that sail curves.

The tentacles below can reach ten metres. They catch small crustaceans and fish.

So what do you do when a part of the natural world is difficult to preserve?

Preserving fluid strips a soft, see-through animal of almost all its natural colour. Soft-bodied sea creatures like this one couldn't be dried, or skinned, or preserved convincingly. They were a gap in museum displays.

In Dresden, in Germany, a glassworker named Leopold Blaschka and his son Rudolf crafted a glass-working method that accurately captured the forms of creatures from the natural world. Their models were excellent teaching and research tools. And each one was also a piece of art.

Leopold had a passion for natural history, and practical skill in glassworking. His family business made ornaments, laboratory equipment and glass eyes. Natural history models started as a hobby.

Then the sailing ship he was travelling on was becalmed. Becalmed means stopped. No wind at all for its sails. It gave him time to observe and sketch marine life.

Prince Camille de Rohan ordered a hundred glass orchids. The director of the Dresden Natural History Museum saw them, and ordered twelve sea anemones. They were creatures he had found impossible to display.

By eighteen seventy-eight, the Blaschkas' catalogue listed six hundred and thirty different models. Later, there were more than seven hundred.

The technique is called flameworking. Glass melted and bent with hand tools, at low temperatures. In places, worked into layers thinner than an eggshell.

Look at the tentacles again as you hear that.

Colour was added in three ways. Painted on. Enamelled. Or built in, using coloured glass from the start.

They worked from descriptions and illustrations sent by scientists. And from living animals. The naturalist Anton Dohrn shipped live invertebrates from Naples, in Italy, all the way to Dresden, so the Blaschkas could see them for themselves.

Julius von Haast, this Museum's founding director, placed his order in eighteen eighty-two. The models reached Christchurch in October, eighteen eighty-three.

The Press reviewed the shipment that month. Of this one, the reviewer wrote that it resembles nature so closely as to be deceptive.

The models first went on show in the Technological Room, as industrial art applied to science. By eighteen ninety-five, that room had been dismantled, and the models had been moved in among the zoological displays.

And in the Museum's catalogue today, this one is filed under European Decorative Arts.
```

## Voice notes

Check these on your first render and override with a phoneme tag or a respelling if the
engine gets them wrong:

- **Blaschka** — commonly BLASH-ka
- **Dohrn** — commonly DORN
- **Haast** — commonly HAHST
- **Camille de Rohan** — French; ro-AHN, with a silent final n
- **man o' war** — should read as "man oh war"; respell as "man o war" if the
  apostrophe causes a stumble. It occurs three times
- **zooids** — ZOH-oyds; some engines flatten it to "zoids"

Set the voice to a New Zealand or British English variant. A US voice will stress
"centimetres" differently and may flatten "Christchurch".

## Sourcing

Every fact comes from one of three Canterbury Museum sources: the collection record for
1884.137.33, Rebecca Le Grice's Blaschka collection page, and Shaw et al. 2017, Records
of the Canterbury Museum 31. Full attribution is in man-o-war-object-story.md.

Four lines are editorial rather than sourced. They are instructions to look, not claims:
"Take a moment to look at it", "You can count them", "Look at the top section", and
"Look at the tentacles again as you hear that". They are what makes this an audio guide
rather than a label read aloud, but they are not the Museum's words.

The bridging question, "So what do you do when a part of the natural world is difficult
to preserve?", is Le Grice's own, shortened. It was added because the eight-track version
used a heading to make that turn, and a single track has no headings to do it.

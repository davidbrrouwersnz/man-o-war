# Group panels and endings — first draft

Eleven pages. Each has a **panel** (~50 words, at the head of the page, above the objects) and an
**ending** (one or two sentences, at the foot, before the neighbouring pages).

Voice: the guide, not the Museum (BUILD-SPEC-v2.md §6). Direct address is allowed. These sit above
object descriptions that stay in the catalogue's own words, so the seam between the two voices is
visible on purpose.

Membership: [`src/data/groups.json`](../src/data/groups.json).

---

## 1 · Jellyfish, large and small — 13

**Panel.** A jellyfish is a bell of almost pure water, pulsing, trailing threads that sting. That
is the whole design. Here it runs from one the size of a dinner plate down to others you could
rest on a fingertip — and three times over, the same animal shown as male, as female, and as an
infant.

**Ending.** Everything on this page was a jellyfish. The next one opens with the most famous
jellyfish of all, which turns out to be neither famous for the right reason nor a jellyfish.

---

## 2 · The man o' war and other floating colonies — 8

**Panel.** A Portuguese man o' war is not one animal. It is hundreds, fused into a single drifting
colony — some members sting, some digest, some reproduce — and not one of them could survive
alone. What you are looking at is less a creature than a crew.

**Ending.** These go where the water goes. The next page belongs to animals that solved the same
problem — open water, nowhere to hide — a completely different way.

---

## 3 · Comb jellies and sea butterflies — the see-through swimmers — 9

**Panel.** In open water there is nowhere to hide. So these animals, unrelated and from different
branches of the tree, arrived at the same two answers: become invisible, and swim by beating
something. Glass is the only material that could ever have shown you the first one.

**Ending.** Transparency is a defence. On the next page it becomes a disguise for something else
entirely — a body so plain it hides one of the strangest facts in this collection.

---

## 4 · Sea squirts and salps — 10

**Panel.** They look like nothing much: a jelly sack, pumping seawater in one opening and out the
other. But this is the group that gave rise to us — somewhere in their life cycle sits a swimming,
tadpole-shaped animal with a stiff rod down its back. Of everything in this collection, these
blobs are your closest relatives.

**Ending.** You arrived on this page still drifting and you leave it fixed to a rock. Everything
from here lives on the seabed.

---

## 5 · Squid, cuttlefish and the paper nautilus — 7

**Panel.** Molluscs that gave up the shell and became hunters instead: arms, suckers, and an eye
very nearly as good as yours. The argonaut is here twice, deliberately. The female builds a paper
cradle for her eggs. The male is barely a centimetre long.

**Ending.** Losing the shell bought speed, arms and eyes. On the next page, the same decision was
paid for very differently.

---

## 6 · Sea slugs — 14

**Panel.** Take a snail's shell away and it needs another answer. These fourteen found several:
poison, colour loud enough to work as a warning, stinging cells stolen from the animals they eat.
One goes further and steals the chloroplasts out of the algae it eats, keeps them working, and
runs partly on sunlight.

**Ending.** Every animal so far has moved under its own power. From here, most of them stay where
they are.

---

## 7 · Sea anemones — 19

**Panel.** An anemone is a stomach on a foot with a ring of poisoned tentacles on top. That is the
entire animal. These nineteen show how far one simple idea can be pushed. It is the largest group
here, and that is not an accident: anemones were the great Victorian obsession, and the one thing
nobody could keep in a jar.

**Ending.** One animal, nineteen ways. Next: what happens when that same animal stops being one
and becomes thousands.

---

## 8 · Corals, sea fans and hydroids — animals that grow like plants — 15

**Panel.** Every branch, fan and stony twig on this page is a city. Each is thousands of tiny
anemone-like animals sharing a single skeleton — which is how they grow like plants without being
plants, and why a coral can be red, blue, feathered, or turned entirely to stone.

**Ending.** These built upward from the rock. The last two pages of the sea belong to animals that
went the other way, down into it.

---

## 9 · Sea cucumbers, feather stars and brittle stars — 8

**Panel.** A sausage, a feather and a star. Nothing about these eight looks related, and that is
the point: count in fives, look for hundreds of tiny water-powered feet, and they turn out to be
built on a starfish's plan — even the two that look like nothing but a worm. There is not one
starfish among the models here, and the reason is the whole reason this collection exists: a
starfish dries and keeps. These do not.

**Ending.** Built like a starfish, shaped like anything but. The next page is a whole group
defined the same way — by a solution rather than a family.

---

## 10 · Worms of the seabed — 14

**Panel.** A worm is not a family. It is a solution — a long soft body for pushing through
sediment — arrived at separately by four different branches of the animal kingdom. Three here hide
the body in a tube altogether and put out a crown so fine it was long mistaken for a flower.

**Ending.** That is the sea. One page left, and not one animal on it ever saw it.

---

## 11 · Never went to sea — 10

**Panel.** Ten of the models in this collection never saw salt water: garden slugs, a Roman snail cut open to show its
insides, a leech from a pond. The Blaschkas supplied classrooms as well as museums, and a lecture
room needed the animal in the cabbage patch as much as the one in the Bay of Naples.

**Ending.** Two men in a landlocked city built an ocean out of illustrations, letters, and living
animals shipped to them alive from the Bay of Naples. These ten are the only ones they could have
walked outside and caught.

---

## Notes on the draft

- **Page 9's panel is doing the most work** and knows it. The page is the scheme's weakest — the
  eight objects share nothing visible — so the panel turns that into the hook rather than
  apologising for it, and lands on a genuine curiosity: there is no starfish in the collection.
- **The endings chain.** Each names what you just saw and sets up the next page's turn. Read in
  sequence they carry the descent — surface, mid-water, rock, sediment, out of the sea — without
  ever announcing it.
- **Both load-bearing facts are now verified against the resolved taxonomy [V]:**
  - **No starfish *here*.** Zero of the 127 are class Asteroidea. The eight echinoderms are only
    Holothuroidea, Crinoidea and Ophiuroidea — sea cucumbers, feather stars, brittle stars.
    **An earlier draft of this panel said "The Blaschkas never made one." That is a claim about
    ~700 catalogued models sold worldwide, and nothing in this project touches it** — Ward's
    1878/1888 catalogues would settle it in an afternoon and have not been checked. The panel says
    *not one here*, and must never say more than that until someone reads Ward.
  - **The solar-powered slug is 1884.137.76, *Elysia chlorotica*** — the textbook case of a sea
    slug retaining functioning chloroplasts. There is a second sacoglossan (1884.137.87,
    *Polybranchia orientalis*), but functional retention varies across the group, so the panel now
    says **one**, not two. Name *Elysia* in the object text rather than the panel.
- **Word counts** run 46–55. Tighten to a consistent band once we have settled the wording.
- **These drafts predate the read-and-heard standard (BUILD-SPEC-v2.md §6) and do not meet it.**
  They were written for the page. Under §13 the spoken words are the printed words, so every panel
  and ending is also an audio script, and two problems follow:
  - **Idiom that carries a whole sentence.** *"less a creature than a crew"* (2), *"That is the
    sea"* (10), *"a stomach on a foot"* (7), *"'Worm' is not a family. It is a solution"* (10).
    These are the best lines on the page and the worst input for twelve translations (§7). Keep
    the meaning in the plain clause; let the figure be a bonus, not the load-bearing part.
  - **Endings that assert what the reader just did.** *"You arrived on this page still drifting"*
    (4) is false for a visitor who arrived by QR code, which §11 says is the normal case. Every
    panel and ending must read correctly for someone who arrived here directly and will read no
    other page. The chain is the second reading, not the first.
